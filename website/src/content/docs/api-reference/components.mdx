## WorldTransform

Cached world-space transform, derived from a [`Transform`] plus the chain of ancestor `Transform`s reached through [`Parent`] references.  Recomputed each frame by [`crate::ecs::hierarchy::resolve_world_transforms`]. Sprite rendering uses this when present; behavior systems and physics still mutate the local [`Transform`].  A world transform is a full 2D affine map: a local point `p` lands at `position + R(rotation) * K(shear) * (scale * p)`, where `K(k)` is `[[1, k], [0, 1]]`. `shear` is zero unless a turned child sits under a non-uniformly scaled (or sheared) parent - a rune circle spinning on a floor squashed into perspective. Before Sep 2026 there was no shear, so such a child turned as a whole ellipse instead of turning *within* its parent's squash (Godot's `Transform2D` and Unity's matrices both shear). See [`crate::ecs::hierarchy::compose`].  Foundation slice of `docs/feature-backlog.md` item 3.5; the bone / animation / Spine slices build on top.

|-------|------|-------------|
| `position` | `Vec2` |  |
| `rotation` | `f32` | Rotation in radians. |
| `scale` | `Vec2` |  |
| `shear` | `f32` | Shear between the scale and the rotation (see the type's docs). |

## Parent

Entity-parenting link. Attaching this to a child entity tells the hierarchy system to compose the child's local [`Transform`] under the parent's [`WorldTransform`] each frame.  Stored as a `hecs::Entity` for direct lookup at runtime. Scene serialization writes it out as a *name reference* (the parent entity's `name` field) and resolves back to an `Entity` on load — see `crate::scene::format::load_scene`.  Cycles are detected and broken at resolution time (a cycle leaves the child's `WorldTransform` equal to its local `Transform` for that frame and emits a `log::warn!`).

Newtype wrapper around `hecs::Entity`.

## IkTwoBone

Slice 3.5.F — two-bone IK constraint attached to the *end* (effector) bone of a 3-bone chain `root → mid → end`. The solver walks `Parent` twice from the entity carrying this component to find the `mid` and `root` bones, then rotates `root.Transform.rotation` and `mid.Transform.rotation` (in local space) so that the chain's tip reaches `target` (world-space). Bone lengths come from the `Bone` component's `length` field.  `flip_bend` selects the "elbow up" vs "elbow down" solution.  Scaled and mirrored rigs work (Sep 2026, BUGS #152): the lengths are scaled by each bone's world `scale.x`, and under a mirrored parent (`scale.x * scale.y < 0`, the usual way a 2D character turns round) `flip_bend` keeps its meaning on screen - the knee goes to the mirrored side, as the rest of the character does.  `weight` (Sep 2026) blends the solve over the animated pose: 0 leaves the clip's leg alone, 1 is all IK - so a foot can be planted and released. `ground` plants an **animated** foot on what is under it: the target is then not a script's point but the foot where the clip put it, moved up or down onto the colliders beneath (`ecs::outlines`) by as much as the ground there is above or below the clip's own floor. A walk authored on flat ground walks up a slope; the body is the game's to raise.

| `target` | `[f32; 2]` |  |

| `weight` | `f32` | How much of the solve to apply, 0..1. |
| `ground` | `Option<IkGround>` | Plant the animated foot on the ground under it (see [`IkGround`]). |

## IkChain

A chain of any number of bones that bends to reach a target (Oct 2026; FABRIK - Godot's `SkeletonModification2DFABRIK`, Unity's 2D IK Chain). On the chain's **end** bone: `bones` counts up the parents from it, so 4 bends this bone and the three above it. The tip - the end bone's far end - goes to `target`, or to the entity named `target_name` when that is set. `max_bend` limits each joint after the first to that many radians either way from where the animation put it (pi is free), which is what keeps a tail from folding back on itself. `weight` blends the solve over the animated pose, as two-bone IK's does. `Skeleton.set_ik_target` / `set_ik_weight` reach it.

## Mesh2D

A textured polygon: what a sprite is, with the corners free.  Drawn **in the sprite pass**, sorted with the sprites, shadows and world text by `z_order`, batched with sprites of the same texture, and put through the entity's `MaterialComponent` like a sprite. That is the difference from `VectorPath` (flat colour, its own pass, always over every sprite) and from `SkinnedMesh` (needs a skeleton, its own pass), and it is why a mesh can be a wall in a first-person corridor with a monster sprite standing in front of it. `SpriteComponent::corners` is the four-vertex, one-colour, affine-textured special case of this.  Nothing in the engine writes the vertices: a package or a script does, per frame if it likes. That is the point - a corridor's walls, a flag in the wind and a jelly are all "a system that writes a `Mesh2D`".  A mesh is never a backdrop: it is not drawn beneath the tilemaps however low its z, because the tilemap pass draws sprites only.

## CameraFollow

Camera follow component — makes the camera track this entity, or **frame a whole set of them**.

**Scene JSON key:** `camera_follow`

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `smoothing` | `f32` | How fast the camera closes on its target, as a **fraction of the remaining distance per frame at 60fps**. Typical: 0.1-0.3. So **larger is faster**: `1.0` snaps instantly and `0.0` never moves at all. This doc comment used to say the opposite - "0 = instant snap, 1 = very slow follow" - which is exactly backwards, and the kind of error that costs an afternoon: a scene authored against it gets a camera that does not move and reads as a broken follow rather than a mistuned one. The arithmetic (`1 - (1 - smoothing)^(dt * 60)`) has always worked this way. |
| `offset` | `Vec2` | Camera offset from the entity position (in world units). |
| `bounds` | `Option<[f32; 4]>` | Optional bounds: [min_x, min_y, max_x, max_y]. Camera won't move beyond these. |
| `frame_tag` | `Option<String>` | Frame **every entity carrying this tag** instead of following the one this component is on: centre on their bounding box and zoom out until it fits. Following one entity is the wrong shape for a whole genre. A two-player duel, a co-op screen, a boss and the player who is running from it - each has a *set* of things that must all stay visible, and none of them is served by picking one to centre on. The arcade needed it for a ship melee, where both ships are constantly running away from each other. With no tagged entity in the world it falls back to following this entity, so a scene does not go blank when the things it was framing are destroyed. |
| `frame_padding` | `f32` | World units of margin around the framed set, so the things being framed are not flush against the edge of the screen. |
| `zoom_range` | `Option<[f32; 2]>` | `[min, max]` zoom while framing. Zoom here is the renderer's: **a larger number is closer in**, so `min` is how far out the camera may pull and `max` is how far in it may push. The max matters more than it looks. Without a ceiling, two ships drifting together zoom the camera until the screen is two sprites and a wall of empty space, which is disorienting and hides everything either of them is about to run into. |
| `dead_zone` | `[f32; 2]` | A box round the middle of the view, `[width, height]` in world units, inside which the target moves without the camera following (Oct 2026; Godot's drag margins, Cinemachine's dead zone). Past its edge the camera is dragged along so the target sits on the edge. `[0, 0]` (the default) follows every step, as before. |
| `look_ahead` | `f32` | Look ahead: aim this many **seconds** ahead of where the target is going, by its velocity - so a runner sees what it runs into rather than what it left. 0 (the default) is off. |
| `look_ahead_max` | `f32` | The furthest the look-ahead may lead, in world units. |
| `look_ahead_smoothing` | `f32` | How fast the look-ahead swings round when the target turns: a fraction of the way per frame at 60 fps, like `smoothing`. Slow, so a turn does not throw the view. |

## RigidBody2D

Rigid body component — attaches a physics body to an entity.

**Scene JSON key:** `rigid_body`

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `body_type` | `BodyType` |  |
| `mass` | `f32` |  |
| `gravity_scale` | `f32` |  |
| `linear_damping` | `f32` |  |
| `angular_damping` | `f32` |  |
| `fixed_rotation` | `bool` |  |
| `ccd` | `bool` | Sweep this body's shape along its path instead of only testing where it landed - **continuous collision detection**. Rapier integrates velocity and then looks for overlaps, so a body that crosses more than its own thickness in one step can be past a wall by the time anything is measured: no contact is generated and it simply leaves. A 12-unit ball at 3000 units a second covers 50 units in a 60 Hz step. It is wrong for anything that is *supposed* to keep moving. ## CharacterController2D

The entity needs a kinematic `RigidBody2D` and a `Collider2D`; a capsule or a box with rounded corners slides best.  The physics step fills in the rest of the fields after it moves the body. ## DirectionalLight2D

## AmbientOcclusion

**Ambient occlusion**: the ambient light darkened near the outlines of `LightOccluder`s, as it is where two surfaces meet - the soft darkening at the foot of a wall, in a corner, between two boxes set close. Put it on any one entity (the first found is used), beside the `ambient_light`.  A point `d` from an occluder's outline keeps `1 - strength * (1 - d/radius)^2` of the ambient light from that occluder, and nothing within `radius` of none; near two occluders the two multiply, so a corner or a narrow gap goes darker than one wall. A point *inside* an occluder takes none from that occluder - the top of a box is not darkened by its own sides. Only the ambient term is occluded: a lamp still lights a corner. Godot builds the same thing from its occluders' distance field; the showcase's Clay scene is built on it. `renderer::lighting::ambient_occlusion_at` is the rule, on the CPU and (transcribed) on the GPU.

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `radius` | `f32` | How far from an outline the darkening reaches, in world units. |
| `strength` | `f32` | How dark it gets at the outline itself: 0 none, 1 black. |

## GlobalIllumination

**Global illumination** (Oct 2026): light that bounces. The lit outlines of the occluders - the scene's colour times the direct light on them - shine again, and every pixel gathers what reaches it round the occluders between, tinted by what it came off: sun through red glass onto a stone floor warms the walls beside it. Radiance cascades, on the GPU (`renderer::backend::wgpu::gi`), added to the light buffer before the composite. Godot and Unity have no 2D equivalent built in. One per scene, like ambient occlusion; `Lighting.set_global_illumination` sets it. `light_at` leaves bounced light out.

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `strength` | `f32` | How much of the bounce is added: 1 is what reaches each pixel. |
| `scale` | `u32` | Frame pixels to a GI texel each way: the bounce is soft, so it is worked out small. 4 by default; 2 is finer and four times the work. |
| `cascades` | `u32` | How many cascades - each reaches four times as far as the last. 0, the default, takes as many as cross the frame. |

## ToonLighting

**Toon lighting**: the light that reaches each pixel is stepped into `bands` levels instead of falling off smoothly - cel shading. A normal-mapped sphere under one light shows a lit face, a mid band and a shadow band with crisp edges between them, as an anime or comic render does. Put it on any one entity (the first found is used).  Applied in the lighting composite to the light buffer - ambient, lights and lightmaps summed - by its brightest channel, so a light's colour is kept. `light_at` applies the same rule (`renderer::lighting::toon_step`).  Each point goes to its **nearest** band, so light under half a band - an ambient below `1 / (2 * bands)` - is black: hard cel shadows. For a lit shadow band, keep the ambient above that (0.17 for three bands).

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `bands` | `u32` | How many steps light takes between dark and full (at 1.0). Above 1.0 in an HDR scene the steps continue. |
| `softness` | `f32` | How soft the edge between two bands is, as a fraction of a band: `0` is a hard line, `1` is smooth again. |
| `dither` | `f32` | Dithered lighting: the size of a dither cell in **world units**, `0` for none. The `softness` of each band's edge is then drawn as an ordered (4x4 Bayer) pattern of the two bands instead of a gradient - the look of a palette-limited retro game, whose light could only come in a few shades. Anchored to the world, so a cell the size of an art pixel stays on that pixel as the camera moves (and lands on the `pixelate` grid when both use it). |

## LightOccluder

Marks an entity as a light blocker. During the lighting pass each occluder contributes world-space edge segments that point and spot lights ray-test against — fragments whose line-of-sight to a light is crossed by any segment receive no (or, with soft shadows, reduced) contribution from that light, producing cast shadows / field-of-view wedges.  Shape resolution priority (highest first): 1. `polygon` — an explicit local-space polygon (CCW, closed    automatically). Each consecutive pair of points is one edge. 2. `from_alpha` — the silhouette of the sprite's opaque pixels, traced    from its texture (`ecs::alpha_shape`); its box until the pixels load. 3. `from_sprite` — derive an axis-aligned box from the entity's    `SpriteComponent` size (or its explicit `size` override). 4. `size` — a plain box `size` world units wide/tall.  All shapes are transformed by the entity's `Transform` (position + rotation + scale).

**Scene JSON key:** `light_occluder`

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `enabled` | `bool` | When false the occluder is ignored (casts no shadow). |
| `size` | `[f32; 2]` | Full width/height of the blocking box in world units, before the entity's `Transform.scale` is applied. Used when neither `polygon` nor `from_sprite` apply. |
| `from_sprite` | `bool` | Derive the blocking box from the entity's `SpriteComponent` bounds instead of `size`. |
| `polygon` | `Vec<[f32; 2]>` | Explicit local-space polygon outline (CCW). Overrides `size` / `from_sprite` when non-empty. Needs at least 3 points. |
| `shadow_tint` | `[f32; 3]` | RGB colour the shadow lets through when `shadow_opacity` is high. `[0, 0, 0]` (the default) produces a neutral shadow; a coloured tint like `[1, 0, 0]` makes a translucent occluder pass only the red channel, simulating coloured glass. |
| `shadow_opacity` | `f32` | How opaque the occluder is in `[0, 1]`. `1.0` (default) fully blocks light (tinted by `shadow_tint`); `0.0` casts no shadow at all; values in between produce translucent shadows. The light passing through equals `mix([1,1,1], shadow_tint, shadow_opacity)`. |
| `self_shadow` | `bool` | Whether the occluder shadows **its own inside** - what Unity calls `ShadowCaster2D.selfShadows`. Off (the default since Sep 2026), a point inside the outline takes light as if the occluder were not there, while everything beyond it is shadowed as before - so a lit wall, pillar or crate that casts a shadow is lit itself, and its normal map shades it. Until then every occluder shadowed its inside: the ray from a light to a point inside crosses the outline on its way out, so a lit sprite under its own occluder drew black, and the advice was to draw it `unlit`, or - the showcase's Crypt - to give a column an occluder of zero width. Turn it on for an occluder that stands for something solid whose inside should be dark, like a wall seen from above whose top the light cannot reach. A light *inside* the outline is still boxed in by it: points outside are shadowed, points inside are lit. |
| `shadows_casters` | `bool` | Whether its shadow falls on other **casters** - sprites whose entity has an occluder too. On (the default) is top-down lighting, where a wall shadows the crate beside it. Off is a side view (Sep 2026): a tower's blocks stand in front of the wall behind them, so each casts onto the wall and not onto the blocks beside it - with it on, every stone was in its neighbours' shadow and the tower drew black. The GPU knows a caster's pixels from the normal pass; `light_at` has no pixels and treats every point as not a caster. |
| `shadow_length` | `f32` | How far the shadow reaches past the occluder, in world units; `0` (the default) is unbounded. The last quarter fades out. Lighting here is flat land: every occluder is infinitely tall, so a shadow runs on to the edge of the light. That is right for a wall and wrong for a bench under a far sun, whose shadow in a top-down view is a short patch - unbounded, the first pew in a nave shadows every pew behind it (the showcase's Stained Glass met exactly that). Set it to the length the shadow would have: roughly the object's height over the tangent of the light's elevation. Up to about 8,000 units, in steps of four. |
| `from_alpha` | `bool` | Cast the **shape of the sprite** (Oct 2026): the outline of its opaque pixels, traced from the texture - a tree's crown and trunk, a fence's pickets - rather than its box. Each island of the sprite is a loop; holes are left out. |
| `alpha_cutoff` | `f32` | The alpha above which a pixel is part of the shape (0..1). |
| `alpha_tolerance` | `f32` | How far, in texels, the traced outline may stray from the pixels' edge - larger is fewer corners. Every corner is an edge each nearby light spends from its pool of 448, so a loop is also held to 48. |
| `traced` | `Option<crate::ecs::alpha_shape::TracedShape>` | The traced outline, kept until the sprite's texture, region, size or anchor change (`ecs::alpha_shape::trace_occluder_shapes`). Never saved. |

## DespawnOffscreen

Despawn this entity once it has left the view.  Every scrolling game needs this and, until Sep 2026, every one of them wrote it again in Lua: walk your own list of spawned things, compare each one against the camera, despawn the ones behind. `Lifetime` covers "this goes away after a while"; this covers "this goes away once nobody can see it", which is the other half.  Not [`DespawnOutOfBounds`], which is a **fixed** world-space box: in a game whose world scrolls, that box would have to move with the view, and there is nothing to move it. **Scene JSON key:** `despawn_offscreen`

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|

| `behind_only` | `bool` | Only despawn once it is *behind* the camera's travel - what a scrolling game wants, because whatever is ahead has not had its turn yet. ## Trail

A **trail**: a ribbon left behind the entity as it moves - a sword's swing, a comet's tail, a light painted with the mouse. Unity's `TrailRenderer`, Godot's `Line2D` fed from a script.  Every frame of play the entity's world position is recorded once it has moved `min_distance` from the last point (`ecs::trails::tick_trails`, which every runtime runs right after resolving the hierarchy); a point lives `lifetime` seconds. `Trail.set_emitting` and `Trail.clear` steer it from a script. Long gaps between points - a fast swing - are drawn as curves, not chords. The ribbon runs from the entity's position this frame back through the points, its width and colour going from `width` / `color` at the head to `width_end` / `color_end` at a point's last moment - so with a `color_end` of alpha 0 or a `width_end` of 0 a point fades out rather than popping off the tail. The texture is stretched along the whole ribbon: `u` 0 at the head and 1 at the tail, `v` across.  Drawn in the sprite pass (sorted by `z_order` with sprites, lit unless `Unlit`, blended by `blend_mode`). A trail on a child is the trail of where the child is - put one on the tip of a sword, not the hilt.

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `lifetime` | `f32` | How long a point lives, in seconds - the length of the tail in time. |
| `width` | `f32` | Width at the head, in world units. |
| `width_end` | `f32` | Width at a point's last moment. |
| `color` | `[f32; 4]` | Colour (linear RGBA) at the head. |
| `color_end` | `[f32; 4]` | Colour at a point's last moment. |

## Oscillator

## BulkEntity

Marks an entity spawned in bulk by a system, rather than authored or individually meaningful.  Three things skip it, for the same reason — nobody addresses it one at a time:  1. **The per-frame script mirror.** The browser-script hosts copy every    entity's transform into JS each frame so a script *could* call    `get_position(e)`. Marked entities are left out. 2. **The editor's Scene panel.** The panel renders one DOM row per entity    with several event handlers, and it does not virtualise — 16,000 boids    meant ~16,000 rows and roughly 68 ms a frame of layout, against 24 ms of    actual engine work. A list that long is useless to a human anyway.  3. **Scene snapshots.** `push_scene` / `pop_scene` and the start of a play    session serialize the whole world, and that cost is a hitch mid-game    rather than something a save dialog pays. Marked entities are left out    by [`crate::scene::snapshot_scene_v2`]: they belong to a spawner that    recreates them, so writing 16,000 boids into a snapshot preserves state    nobody addressed individually. Measured at 16,000: **40.05 ms -> 0.35    ms**. A real *save* still keeps every entity — dropping one there would    be data loss.  Put it on flocks, projectiles, debris — anything a system creates by the thousand. Simulation and rendering are completely unaffected.  The trade is exact: a marked entity's slot in the script mirror is never filled, so `get_position(e)` reads the zeroed default rather than its real transform; it cannot be selected in the editor; and it does not survive a scene push. If you need to read, move or inspect an entity individually — or to have it come back after a `pop_scene` — do not mark it.  (Named `NoScriptSync` when first added; renamed once it also governed editor visibility, since the old name described only a third of what it does.)

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `radius` | `f32` | Soft mutual separation: entities carrying this gently push apart from each other when overlapping (crowd spacing). `apply_separation` only considers other `Separation` entities, so the player / props are unaffected unless they opt in. Personal-space radius; a pair pushes apart while closer than the sum of their radii. |

## Flicker

Periodically toggles `SpriteComponent.color` alpha between `0.0` and the entity's pre-flicker alpha at `frequency` toggles per second, for a fixed `duration`. Auto-removes itself when `elapsed >= duration` and restores the original alpha.  Canonical use: post-hit "iframe" damage flash on a player or enemy. Requires a `SpriteComponent` on the same entity (skipped silently otherwise).

**Scene JSON key:** `flicker`

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `frequency` | `f32` | Toggles per second (e.g. `10.0` = 5 on, 5 off per second). |
| `duration` | `f32` | Total flicker time in seconds. Component is removed after this. |
| `elapsed` | `f32` | Time elapsed inside this flicker. Initialise to `0.0`. |
| `original_alpha` | `Option<f32>` | Captured alpha at flicker start. `None` means "capture from `SpriteComponent.color[3]` on first tick" — same self-priming pattern as `Oscillator.center`. |

## Shake

Transient additive jitter offset applied to `Transform.position` for `duration` seconds. Intensity decays linearly from `intensity` at start to `0` at end. Auto-removes itself when expired and restores the entity to its pre-shake position.  Canonical use: hit-reaction camera shake or impact-frame entity shake. The shake is sampled at `frequency` Hz with linear interpolation between samples so the motion stays jittery rather than a smooth sine.  Pure: mutates only `Transform.position`, and only by the jitter: each tick removes last tick's offset and adds a new one, so whatever else moves the entity keeps moving it. When the shake ends the offset is removed.

**Scene JSON key:** `shake`

| Field | Type | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| `intensity` | `f32` | Peak displacement magnitude in world units at start. |
| `frequency` | `f32` | New random offset samples per second. |
| `duration` | `f32` | Total shake duration in seconds. |
| `elapsed` | `f32` | Time elapsed inside this shake. Initialise to `0.0`. |
| `offset` | `Option<[f32; 2]>` | Offset applied on the last tick, removed before the next is applied. Runtime state; `None` until the first tick. Replaces a captured `origin` the entity was pinned to every tick, which froze anything moving - a knocked-back enemy, a walking player - for the length of the shake and then snapped it back to where the shake began. Scenes that serialized `origin` still load; it is ignored. |
| `seed` | `Option<u32>` | Internal PRNG state seeded on first tick — kept on the component so each entity has its own deterministic stream. |

## Enums

### SpriteFill

How a sprite's drawn area is filled with its source texture.  Border / tile-size values are expressed in the **same units as `SpriteComponent::size`** (world units).  A nine-slice border means the same number of **texture pixels**: a 16-unit border draws the texture's outer 16 pixels 16 units wide, however large the sprite. So the corners keep their shape and only the edges and centre stretch. The texture's size is looked up once it is loaded (`NineSlice::source_px`, filled by `resolve_sprite_regions`); until then the sprite draws stretched.  Until Sep 2026 the UV slice was `border / size` - the *drawn* size - which makes every cell sample exactly the part of the texture a plain stretch would. NineSlice drew identically to Stretch (test-lab BUGS #96).
